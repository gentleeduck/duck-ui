Every comparison table lies a little, usually by omitting what it did not test. This page separates two kinds of claim: things measured in-tree against a library that is actually installed, and things read from another project's documentation. No number here is a benchmark result; those live on the [benchmarks](/duck-iam/benchmarks) page.

## What this page compares against

**Measured.** Five libraries are installed as devDependencies of `@gentleduck/iam` and benchmarked head to head in `test/benchmark.bench.ts`. Every performance claim about them comes from running that file:

| Library | Version benchmarked |
| --- | --- |
| `@casl/ability` | `^7.0.0` |
| `casbin` | `^5.50.0` |
| `accesscontrol` | `^3.1.0` |
| `@rbac/rbac` | `^2.1.3` |
| `easy-rbac` | `^4.0.0` |

**Not measured.** Oso, OPA, Cedar, Permit.io, and Cerbos are not installed and not benchmarked. They appear below only on feature axes taken from their own documentation, and no ops-per-second figure is claimed for any of them. Where a comparison is documentation-derived, the table says so.

## The space

TypeScript and Node authorization tools cluster into four archetypes, and the archetype matters more than any individual feature.

<MermaidDiagram chart={`flowchart TD
    ROOT["Authorization tooling"] --> DSL["1. DSL engines"]
    ROOT --> JSON["2. JSON or object policy engines"]
    ROOT --> CODE["3. Code-first builders"]
    ROOT --> SVC["4. Hosted policy services"]

    DSL --> DSL1["OPA with Rego, AWS Cedar"]
    JSON --> JSON1["Casbin, AccessControl.js"]
    CODE --> CODE1["CASL, Oso, duck-iam"]
    SVC --> SVC1["Permit.io, Cerbos Cloud"]`} />

duck-iam is archetype 3, with one twist: RBAC and ABAC are not separate subsystems. Roles compile into a synthetic policy and meet your hand-written policies inside the same evaluator, under the same [combining algorithms](/duck-iam/core/cross-policy). You do not choose one model; you write both side by side.

## Where the decision happens

The archetypes differ most in where the check runs and what a policy change costs.

<MermaidDiagram chart={`flowchart LR
    subgraph inproc["In-process, compiled at build"]
        CASL["CASL: rules frozen at build(), hash lookup"]
    end

    subgraph inproc2["In-process, loaded at runtime"]
        DUCK["duck-iam: adapter loads catalog, LRU caches, invalidator"]
        CASBIN["Casbin: model file plus adapter or watcher"]
    end

    subgraph remote["Out of process"]
        OPA["OPA or Cedar sidecar: network hop per decision"]
        HOST["Hosted service: network hop plus vendor"]
    end

    REQ["Request needs a decision"] --> CASL
    REQ --> DUCK
    REQ --> CASBIN
    REQ --> OPA
    REQ --> HOST`} />

`CASL` is the fastest of these because it does the least at request time: rules are frozen at `build()` and every check is one hash lookup. `DUCK` and `CASBIN` accept a runtime cost in exchange for policies that change without a redeploy. `OPA` and `HOST` trade microseconds for a network round trip, and get language independence in return.

That framing explains most of the benchmark gap: duck-iam is measurably slower than CASL per check and measurably faster than Casbin, and both results follow from where each one does its work.

Two smaller libraries appear in the benchmark suite without a column here, because their surface is too narrow for the axes above. `easy-rbac` is roughly 2 KB and needs no configuration beyond a hierarchical role object: a good fit when hierarchical RBAC is the entire requirement, and it benchmarks faster than duck-iam's own `iamEvaluateFast()`. `@rbac/rbac` is around 4 KB, allows role updates at runtime, and ships Express, NestJS, and Fastify middleware. Neither supports attribute conditions, so neither appears in the ABAC scenarios. Both size figures are bundlephobia numbers checked 2026-03-30.

## When to pick duck-iam

- You want compile-time errors on misspelled action, resource, role, and scope names.
- You need RBAC and ABAC together and do not want to maintain two policy systems that combine by hand.
- You need multi-tenant [scoped roles](/duck-iam/core/roles/scoped): the same subject holding different roles per organization.
- You need to answer "why was this denied?" in production support, and [`explain()`](/duck-iam/advanced/explain) gives you the rule, the condition, and the actual versus expected values.
- Your policies change at runtime, written by an admin UI or promoted between environments through [`engine.admin.export()`](/duck-iam/advanced/engine/admin).
- You want the operability surface (health probes, cache statistics, cross-instance invalidation, adapter timeouts) without building it.

## When to pick something else

- **OPA or Cedar** when policies must be enforced from more than one language. duck-iam is TypeScript only, and a shared policy set across a Go and a Python service is exactly what a DSL sidecar is for.
- **CASL** when your rules are static, your bundle budget is tight, or your main need is scoping ORM queries. CASL has mature TypeORM, Sequelize, and Mongoose integrations, ships around 6 KB, and is faster per check because it compiles at `build()` time.
- **Casbin** when you want a long-established engine with adapters for practically every database and language, and stringly-typed models are acceptable.
- **A hosted policy service** such as Permit.io or Cerbos Cloud when you would rather not run a policy runtime at all and want a managed editing UI.
- **Nothing at all** when your app has three roles and no conditions. A `switch` statement is a legitimate answer, and adding a policy engine to it is a cost with no return.

## What duck-iam is not

- **Not authentication.** It answers what a subject may do, never who they are. Pair it with [duck-auth](/duck-iam/guides/auth-bridge).
- **Not an audit log or SIEM.** The hooks are first-class and every decision can be observed, but you bring the sink.
- **Not a permission taxonomy.** There is no built-in `manage:user` vocabulary; you declare your own actions and resources.
- **Not a policy editor.** The admin API and JSON schema are there so you can build one; the UI is yours.
- **Not language-agnostic.** The policy format is JSON and portable, but the evaluator is TypeScript.

## Gotchas

<Callout tone="info" title="Read comparison tables for what they omit">
Feature rows are not weighted. Casbin's twenty-plus adapters matter more than duck-iam's six if you are on a database duck-iam has no adapter for, and the "one pipeline" row matters not at all if you only ever needed RBAC. Match the row to your problem before counting them.
</Callout>

- The compiled table addresses roles with a 32-bit mask, so a catalog over 32 roles cannot be compiled. The engine warns once and falls back to the interpreter in both modes: verdicts unchanged, the O(1) lookup gone. A real limit that no feature table would show.
- Competitor versions above are the ones the benchmark suite pins. A newer release of any of them can move the numbers, so re-run `bun run bench` rather than trusting the table's age.
